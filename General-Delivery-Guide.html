Part 1 — Agile, Scrum and Kanban

Software development life cycle (SDLC) and delivery models Basic

The SDLC is the set of stages work passes through: requirements → design → build → test → release → operate → improve. Delivery models differ in how they sequence those stages and how often they loop.

Why so many models?Because uncertainty differs. When requirements are fixed and change is costly (bridges, regulated hardware) a plan-driven model works. When the problem is only understood by building and showing it (most software products) short feedback loops beat a perfect upfront plan.
ModelHow it worksStrengthsWeaknessesUse when
WaterfallPhases run once, in order, with sign-off gatesClear milestones, heavy documentation, simple to contractLate feedback, changes are expensive, risk discovered at the endStable, well-understood scope; compliance-heavy projects
V-modelWaterfall where each phase has a matching test levelTest planning from day oneSame rigidity as waterfallSafety-critical systems
Iterative / IncrementalBuild in slices, each adds working featuresEarly working softwareNeeds architecture disciplineMedium uncertainty
Agile (Scrum, XP)Short time-boxed iterations; continuous feedback and re-planningAdapts to change, early value, transparencyNeeds engaged product owner and disciplined teamProduct development with evolving requirements
Kanban / LeanContinuous flow, limit work in progressSmooths flow, great for support/opsNo natural cadence for planningMaintenance, support, mixed-priority work
DevOpsDevelopment and operations share ownership; automate build-test-deploy-monitorFast, safe, frequent releasesCultural change, tooling investmentAny team shipping online services
Hybrid ("Water-Scrum-Fall")Upfront budgeting/requirements, agile delivery, formal release gateFits traditional organisationsInherits some weaknesses of bothLarge enterprises and fixed-bid contracts

The Agile Manifesto in one minute

Four values

  • Individuals and interactions over processes and tools
  • Working software over comprehensive documentation
  • Customer collaboration over contract negotiation
  • Responding to change over following a plan

"Over" means the left is valued more, not that the right is worthless.

Principles worth quoting

  • Deliver working software frequently (weeks, not months)
  • Welcome changing requirements, even late
  • Business and developers work together daily
  • Sustainable pace; simplicity; maximise work not done
  • Regularly reflect and adjust (retrospective)
Agile is not "no planning" or "no documentation"Agile teams plan continuously (release, sprint, daily) and document what is needed to build and operate the system. What they avoid is large speculative documents that go stale.

Definition of Ready, Definition of Done and acceptance criteria Mid

Definition of Ready (DoR)

Checklist for a story to enter a sprint:

  • Clear value statement (who / what / why)
  • Acceptance criteria written
  • Small enough for one sprint, estimated
  • Dependencies identified, designs/API contract available
  • Testable; no open critical questions

Definition of Done (DoD)

Quality bar for every item (the Increment's commitment):

  • Code reviewed, merged, builds in CI
  • Unit/integration tests pass; coverage not reduced
  • Acceptance criteria demonstrated
  • No critical static-analysis or security findings
  • Docs/API spec updated, deployed to test, monitoring added
DoD vs acceptance criteriaAcceptance criteria are specific to one story ("login locks after 5 failed attempts"). The DoD is the common quality checklist applied to all stories. A story is done only when both are met.

Common anti-patterns

  • Zombie Scrum: events happen but nothing is inspected or adapted.
  • Sprint as mini-waterfall: design week, build week, test week — nothing integrated until the end.
  • Carrying over half-done stories: they are worth zero until Done; split smaller instead.
  • Velocity as a target: using it to compare teams or reward output makes people inflate points (Goodhart's law).
  • Daily as status report to a manager rather than team planning.
Part 2 — JIRA

JIRA: what it is and how its pieces fit Basic

JIRA (Atlassian) is an issue and project tracker. Everything is an issue that moves through a workflow inside a project, is displayed on a board, and can be searched with JQL. Confluence (docs/wiki) is its usual companion; Bitbucket/GitHub integrate commits and PRs into issues.

Why teams use itOne shared, auditable source of truth for "what are we building, who is on it, what state is it in, and why", which feeds sprint planning, reporting, release notes and compliance traceability.

Running a sprint in JIRA

  1. Refine the backlog: write stories, add acceptance criteria, estimate (story points), link dependencies, rank.
  2. Plan: create sprint, set Sprint Goal, drag items from backlog in capacity limits.
  3. Start sprint: set start and end date. Scope added mid-sprint appears on the burndown as a scope change.
  4. Work the board: move cards, flag impediments, update remaining estimates, link PRs (branch name or commit message contains the key: git commit -m "SHOP-123 add retry").
  5. Review: use the sprint report; release version.
  6. Complete sprint: unfinished issues return to the backlog or next sprint; metrics are recorded.
Good ticket hygieneDescriptive summary starting with a verb, a "why", acceptance criteria, a single clear owner, correct type/priority, linked to an epic and fix version, and no comments hiding decisions (decisions go in the description or Confluence).

Writing a good bug

Summary:   [Checkout] Total shows 0.00 when coupon SAVE10 is applied twice

JQL (JIRA Query Language) Mid

JQL is the SQL-like language behind filters, boards and dashboards: field operator value clauses joined with AND/OR, with an optional ORDER BY.

project = SHOP AND status = "In Progress" AND assignee = currentUser()
issuetype = Bug AND priority in (High, Highest) AND resolution = Unresolved ORDER BY priority DESC, created ASC
sprint in openSprints() AND status != Done
fixVersion = "2.14" AND issuetype in (Story, Bug)
created >= -7d AND reporter = currentUser()                      -- relative dates
status changed to "Done" during (startOfWeek(), now())
assignee is EMPTY AND status = "To Do"
text ~ "timeout" AND labels = payments
"Epic Link" = SHOP-50 OR parent = SHOP-50
issueFunction in linkedIssuesOf("key = SHOP-1", "blocks")          -- ScriptRunner add-on
ElementExamples
Operators= != > < >= <= ~ !~ IN NOT IN IS EMPTY IS NOT EMPTY WAS CHANGED
FunctionscurrentUser() openSprints() closedSprints() startOfWeek() now() membersOf("group")
KeywordsAND OR NOT ORDER BY ASC DESC
Fieldsproject, issuetype, status, priority, assignee, reporter, sprint, labels, component, fixVersion, created, updated, resolution, "Story Points"
Use filters to drive everythingSave a JQL as a filter, then base a board, dashboard gadget, subscription email or automation rule on it. Change the filter once and all of them update.

Part 3 — Requirements engineering

Requirements: what they are and how to gather them Basic

A requirement is a statement of a need that the system must satisfy. Requirements engineering is the continuous activity of discovering, clarifying, documenting, validating and managing those needs.

Why it mattersMost expensive defects are not coding mistakes but wrong or misunderstood requirements. The cost of fixing a misunderstanding grows roughly tenfold with each stage it survives (requirements → design → code → test → production).
TypeMeaningExample
Business requirementWhy the organisation wants this; measurable goalCut checkout abandonment from 30% to 20%
Stakeholder / user requirementWhat users need to accomplishA shopper can pay with a saved card
Functional requirementWhat the system does (behaviour)System sends a confirmation email after payment
Non-functional requirement (NFR / quality attribute)How well it does it95% of API calls respond within 300 ms at 500 rps
ConstraintFixed limits: technology, law, budget, deadlineMust run on Azure; GDPR; go-live by 1 March
Assumption / dependencyThings believed true or needed from othersPayment provider sandbox available by sprint 2

A user story is a short description of a need from the user's viewpoint, intended to start a conversation: Card (the text), Conversation (the discussion), Confirmation (acceptance criteria).

Acceptance criteria: Given / When / Then (Gherkin)

Feature: Apply a discount coupon at checkout

  Background:
    Given the cart contains items worth 100.00

  Scenario: Valid coupon reduces the total
    Given a coupon "SAVE10" worth 10 percent exists
    When the shopper applies "SAVE10"
    Then the total is 90.00

  Scenario: The same coupon cannot be used twice
    Given the shopper already applied "SAVE10"
    When the shopper applies "SAVE10" again
    Then an error "Coupon already applied" is shown

  Scenario Outline: Invalid coupons are rejected

    Then the error "<message>" is shown

      | code    | message             |

Prioritising requirements

MoSCoW

  • Must have: release fails without it
  • Should have: important, workaround exists
  • Could have: nice if time allows
  • Won't (this time): explicitly parked

Rule: keep Musts under ~60% of effort to leave buffer.

Kano model

  • Basic expectations (missing = angry)
  • Performance: more is better
  • Delighters: unexpected joy
  • Delighters become basics over time

Scoring models (RICE, WSJF, value vs effort) are covered in the roadmap part, with a calculator.

Traceability and change control

  • Traceability: each requirement links to a story/epic, design, code (commit/PR), test case and release (JIRA links + fix version). Auditors and impact analysis rely on it.
  • Change control: new or changed scope goes through the Product Owner (or change board in formal projects): assess impact on cost/time/risk, then accept, defer or reject. Scope that creeps silently is scope creep.
  • Baseline a version of the requirements at agreed milestones so changes are visible.
Common requirement smellsAmbiguous words ("fast", "user-friendly", "etc.", "should support"), missing error and permission cases, solution in disguise ("use Redis"), unmeasurable NFRs, conflicting needs from different stakeholders, and no owner.
Part 4 — Estimation

Estimation: what it is and why it is hard Basic

An estimate is a prediction of effort or duration under uncertainty. It is neither a commitment (a promise) nor a target (what the business wants). Mixing the three is the root of most estimation conflict.

Why estimates are usually wrong(1) Humans are optimistic and anchor on the happy path. (2) Unknown unknowns: requirements, integration and bugs reveal themselves later. (3) Work is not uniform: waiting, meetings, reviews and context switching eat time. (4) Parkinson's law and the student syndrome stretch work to fit. (5) Pressure: estimates get negotiated down.
The Cone of Uncertainty
Initial conceptactual may be 0.25x - 4x of estimate
→
Approved requirements0.5x - 2x
→
Design complete0.67x - 1.5x
→
Mid-build0.8x - 1.25x
→
Nearly doneabout 1x
ConceptMeaning
Effort vs durationEffort = person-hours of work. Duration = calendar time (effort ÷ people ÷ availability). 10 person-days is not 2 calendar days with 5 people (Brooks' law: adding people to a late project makes it later).
Absolute vs relativeAbsolute: hours/days. Relative: points or T-shirt sizes compared to a reference story. Relative is faster and avoids false precision.
Top-down vs bottom-upTop-down: analogy or parametric from similar projects. Bottom-up: break into tasks and sum. Use both and compare.
Accuracy vs precision"137.5 hours" is precise but probably inaccurate; "3-5 weeks" is honest.
Buffer / contingencyExplicit reserve for risk; add at project level (shared) rather than padding every task.

Agile estimation: story points, planning poker and T-shirt sizes Basic

Story points measure the relative size of a story combining effort, complexity, uncertainty and risk – not hours. A 5 is "about 2.5 times a 2". They are team-specific and meaningless across teams.

PointsTypical meaningExample
1Trivial, well understoodChange a label, add a field to a form
2Small, straightforwardAdd a validated input and persist it
3Some thinking, a few touch pointsNew filter on a list (UI + API + query)
5Moderate complexity or unknownsIntegrate a simple third-party API
8Large; consider splittingNew CRUD module with permissions
13+Too big or too uncertain: split or spike"Build reporting"

The scale is usually Fibonacci-like (1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13...) because uncertainty grows with size; the widening gaps stop false precision ("is it 6 or 7?").

Planning Poker

  1. PO reads the story; team asks clarifying questions.
  2. Everyone privately picks a card, then all reveal at once (avoids anchoring).
  3. High and low voters explain their assumptions (usually they imagined different scope or risk).
  4. Re-vote until close; take the agreed value or the higher one. If it will not converge, split the story or do a spike.

Time-based techniques: WBS, analogy and three-point (PERT) Mid

When a customer needs a date or a budget (fixed-bid, procurement), time-based estimates are required. Break the work down (WBS – Work Breakdown Structure) until each item is small enough to estimate with confidence, then combine with ranges.

Three-point estimate

Ask for Optimistic, Most likely, Pessimistic.

  • Triangular: (O + M + P) / 3
  • PERT: (O + 4M + P) / 6
  • Standard deviation: (P - O) / 6
  • Project SD = √(sum of task variances)

Analogy / parametric

  • Analogy: "the last payment integration took 3 weeks"; adjust for differences.
  • Parametric: units x rate ("40 screens x 2 days"), or function points / use-case points.
  • Delphi: anonymous rounds of expert estimates converging after discussion.

Estimating a whole project: a practical recipe

  1. List scope (epics → stories) and explicitly list exclusions and assumptions.
  2. Estimate each item with O/M/P or points; include all disciplines: development, code review, testing, defects, documentation, environments, deployment, security review, UAT support, project management.
  3. Add risk contingency for known risks (third-party delays, unfamiliar tech): usually 10-30%.
  4. Convert to calendar time using realistic capacity (see next section).
  5. Present a range with confidence levels and the assumptions that would change it. Re-estimate at milestones as the cone narrows.
The pocket formula many leads useDevelopment effort × 1.3 for testing and defects × 1.15 for reviews/documentation × 1.2 for coordination = roughly 1.8× the "pure coding" number. Calibrate it with your own history.

Communicating estimates well

  • Give a range plus confidence: "6-9 weeks, 80% confident, assuming the vendor API is ready by week 2."
  • State assumptions and risks; list what is not included.
  • Do not give a number in the corridor. Say "I will come back after a short breakdown".
  • Re-estimate when facts change and report early when the date is at risk. Bad news does not improve with age.
  • If told "can you do it in 4 weeks?", answer with trade-offs: reduce scope (MoSCoW), add time, or add risk. Do not silently absorb it with overtime.

Release planning steps

  1. Goal: what outcome must this release achieve, and by when does it matter (event, contract, regulation)?
  2. Scope: build a story map, slice vertically, mark the MVP line (MoSCoW).
  3. Size: epics in T-shirts/points, forecast with velocity (range).
  4. Sequence: risky and high-value items first; respect dependencies; leave a hardening/buffer sprint.
  5. Plan the path to production: environments, feature flags, migration, data, training, support hand-over, rollback.
  6. Track with burnup and risk burndown; replan every sprint.

Code review that works

  • Small PRs (< 400 lines), linked to the ticket, with a description of what and why, and screenshots/tests.

Technical debt, documentation and everyday working practices Mid

  • Technical debt is a conscious or accidental shortcut that speeds delivery now and charges "interest" later. Types: deliberate/prudent ("ship now, refactor next sprint"), accidental, and bit-rot (outdated dependencies). Track it as visible backlog items with business impact; reserve a standing capacity share (10-20%); refactor continuously under test (the Boy Scout rule); avoid "big rewrite" proposals.
  • Documentation that earns its keep: README (what, run, test, deploy), architecture overview + ADRs, API docs from OpenAPI, runbooks for operations, onboarding guide. Keep docs next to code and review them in PRs.
  • Definition of good communication: write async updates that stand alone (context, decision needed, deadline); use ticket comments for decisions; summarise meetings with owners and dates.
  • Working in a team: pair/mob programming for hard problems, knowledge sharing sessions, rotate on-call, protect focus time, ask for help after a time-box (for example 30-60 minutes of being stuck).
  • Estimating bugs and support: reserve capacity (for example 15%) instead of estimating every bug; keep a clear triage process with severity and SLAs.

Common delivery and general-engineering interview questions Prep

Filter by topic, expand a question and try to answer it aloud first.

Explain Scrum in two minutes. agile

A framework where a cross-functional team delivers a potentially releasable Increment every sprint (1-4 weeks). A Product Owner orders the backlog, Developers plan and build, a Scrum Master improves the system. Events: Planning, Daily Scrum, Review, Retrospective. Transparency, inspection, adaptation drive it.

Scrum vs Kanban vs Waterfall? agile

Waterfall: sequential phases, late feedback, best for stable scope. Scrum: time-boxed iterations with roles and events, adapts every sprint. Kanban: continuous flow with WIP limits and no prescribed cadence, ideal for support. Choose by uncertainty and nature of work.

What happens if a team keeps missing sprint commitments? agile

Inspect in the retrospective: stories too big, unplanned work, dependencies, unclear acceptance criteria, over-commitment vs real velocity. Plan to average velocity and capacity, slice smaller, protect focus, handle interrupts with a reserved buffer, and track carry-over as a signal not as blame.

What is a good retrospective? agile

Psychologically safe, data-informed (cycle time, escaped bugs), ends with 1-2 owned, time-bound experiments that are reviewed next retro. Vary formats (Start/Stop/Continue, 4Ls, sailboat) to avoid staleness.

How do you handle changing requirements mid-sprint? agile

Protect the Sprint Goal. Urgent changes go through the Product Owner: if the goal is still valid, swap equal-sized items; if the goal is obsolete, the PO may cancel the sprint. Otherwise queue it for the next planning. Make the trade-off visible.

Epic vs Story vs Task vs Sub-task vs Bug? jira

Epic: large body of work across sprints. Story: user-valued slice that fits a sprint (points). Task: non-user-facing work. Sub-task: breakdown of a parent for a single assignee. Bug: defect in existing behaviour with reproduction steps.

How do you customise a JIRA workflow and why would you? jira

In project settings (or admin for company-managed) add statuses and transitions to mirror your real process (e.g. In Review, QA), then add conditions (who can move), validators (required fields) and post-functions (set resolution). Keep it as simple as the team needs; every status is a handoff.

How do you find all high-priority unresolved bugs assigned to me in the current sprint? jira

issuetype = Bug AND priority in (High, Highest) AND resolution = Unresolved AND assignee = currentUser() AND sprint in openSprints(). Save it as a filter and add it to a dashboard.

What do burndown and velocity charts tell you and what can mislead? jira

Burndown shows progress vs the sprint goal; velocity shows capacity trend for forecasting. They mislead when stories are huge (cliff at the end), when scope changes, when points are inflated, or when used to compare teams.

Functional vs non-functional requirements? Give examples. req

Functional: what the system does (send an invoice email). Non-functional: qualities and constraints (p95 latency under 300 ms, 99.9% availability, WCAG AA, GDPR). NFRs often drive architecture and must be measurable and testable.

What makes a good user story? req

INVEST: independent, negotiable, valuable, estimable, small, testable. Written from the user's perspective with a clear "so that", acceptance criteria (Given/When/Then), and linked designs/rules. A vertical slice you can demo.

Requirements are vague or conflicting. What do you do? req

Ask why and who, get concrete examples, prototype or draw the flow, identify the decision-maker (PO), document assumptions, resolve conflicts with data or by escalating, and time-box a spike if technical uncertainty is the blocker. Record decisions in the ticket.

How do you handle scope creep? req

Make every change a visible backlog item with impact on cost/time/risk, route through the PO or change board, trade scope for scope or extend the date, and keep a "later" list so ideas are not lost. Agree a definition of the MVP early.

What is the difference between BRD, PRD and SRS? req

BRD: business goals and needs (why). PRD: product features, personas and metrics (what for users). SRS: detailed software specification of functions and NFRs (what exactly the system does). Agile teams often replace much of this with epics, stories, acceptance criteria and living docs.

How do you estimate a feature you have never built? est

Break it down, compare with similar past work, run a time-boxed spike for the riskiest unknown, estimate with O/M/P ranges, list assumptions and risks, add contingency, and present a range with confidence. Re-estimate as you learn.

Why use story points instead of hours? est

Humans are better at relative than absolute estimates; points include uncertainty and complexity, are independent of who does the work, and avoid false precision. Velocity then converts them to time using real history.

The business asks for a fixed date, and your estimate is longer. What do you say? est

Separate estimate, target and commitment. Show the range and assumptions, then give options: reduce scope (MoSCoW), add capacity (with onboarding costs and Brooks' law), move the date, or accept more risk. Document the choice and track risk weekly.

How do you calculate team capacity for a sprint? est

Person-days minus leave and holidays, ceremonies, support/on-call and a focus factor (about 60-75%); then plan at or below the average velocity of recent sprints, not theoretical capacity.

What is the Cone of Uncertainty? est

Estimates at project start can be off by 4x either way; the variance narrows as requirements, design and implementation reduce unknowns. It argues for ranges early and re-estimation at milestones.

How do you build and maintain a roadmap? road

Start from vision and goals (OKRs), gather input from customers, data, stakeholders and engineering, prioritise with a framework (RICE/WSJF + strategic judgement), express as outcomes in Now/Next/Later, validate feasibility and dependencies with the team, review every quarter, and communicate changes with reasons.

RICE vs WSJF vs MoSCoW – when to use which? road

RICE ranks product features with reach data. WSJF handles mixed work with cost-of-delay (compliance, tech, features). MoSCoW defines scope for a fixed release/MVP. They can be combined: WSJF/RICE to rank, MoSCoW to cut the release.

What is an MVP and how do you decide its scope? road

The smallest end-to-end product that tests the riskiest assumption with real users. Use story mapping to select one thin slice per journey step, apply MoSCoW, define success metrics beforehand, and plan how to learn from it.

How do you manage dependencies on other teams? road

Identify early (planning, dependency board), agree API contracts and dates, mock the dependency, slice work to be independent, track via JIRA links, raise risks at the first sign of slip, and escalate with options rather than complaints.

Trunk-based development vs Git Flow? eng

Trunk-based: tiny short-lived branches, daily merges, feature flags, fast CI: best for continuous delivery. Git Flow: long-lived develop/release branches suited to versioned, scheduled releases but with heavier merging.

Continuous Integration vs Delivery vs Deployment? eng

CI: merge often with automated build and tests. Delivery: every build is releasable, release is a manual decision. Deployment: every passing change goes to production automatically.

What makes a good unit test and what is the test pyramid? eng

Fast, isolated, deterministic, one behaviour, meaningful name, Arrange-Act-Assert. The pyramid prefers many unit tests, fewer integration tests, and a few end-to-end tests for balance of speed and confidence.

Monolith or microservices for a new product? eng

Start with a modular monolith unless there are clear independent scaling/team needs: it is simpler to build, deploy and debug. Extract services later along proven boundaries. Microservices add network, data consistency, observability and operational cost.

How do you reduce technical debt without stopping feature delivery? eng

Make it visible and quantified (time lost, incidents), reserve 10-20% of capacity, refactor opportunistically near changes under tests, prioritise debt that blocks upcoming roadmap items, and avoid big-bang rewrites.

What is the difference between SLI, SLO and SLA? eng

SLI: a measurement (success rate). SLO: internal target for it (99.9%). SLA: contractual promise with penalties. The error budget (100% - SLO) balances reliability and feature speed.

Tell me about a time you disagreed with a teammate or stakeholder. beh

Use STAR: Situation (context, 1-2 sentences), Task (your responsibility), Action (what you did: listened, gathered data, proposed options, agreed on a decision), Result (measurable outcome and what you learned). Show respect and focus on the problem, not the person.

Describe a project that went wrong. What did you learn? beh

Pick a real example with genuine ownership: what signals you missed, what you changed (earlier demos, smaller slices, better risk tracking, monitoring), and the improved result next time. Avoid blaming others.

How do you prioritise when everything is urgent? beh

Clarify impact and deadlines, ask the PO/stakeholders to rank, use cost-of-delay thinking, separate fires from important work, communicate trade-offs and what will slip, and protect a buffer.

How do you handle a tight deadline you believe is unrealistic? beh

Raise it early with data: the estimate range, risks and options (scope cut, more time, more people). Propose an MVP slice that meets the date. Agree and document the decision; do not hide the risk or burn out the team.

Why should we hire you / where do you want to grow? beh

Connect your strongest, proven skills (with one metric-backed example) to the role's needs, show curiosity (a topic you are learning, such as the migration from AngularJS to Angular or cloud-native practices), and align growth goals with what the team offers.

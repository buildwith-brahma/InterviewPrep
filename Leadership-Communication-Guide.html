Part 1 — Communication fundamentals

What good communication actually is Basic

Communication succeeded only if the receiver understood and could act. Sending the message is not the goal. Most failures come from an unclear purpose, the wrong level of detail for the audience, or the wrong channel.

Before you write or speak, answer 3 questions

  • Who is the audience and what do they already know?
  • What do I want them to know, decide or do?
  • When do they need it, and which channel fits?

Match detail to audience

  • Executive: outcome, risk, cost, decision needed
  • Product owner: scope, trade-offs, dates, options
  • Peer developer: design, edge cases, code
  • Customer: impact on them, next step, plain words
PrincipleIn practice
Lead with the point (BLUF)"Bottom line up front": put the conclusion or request in the first sentence, then the reasoning.
One message, one purposeDo not mix an FYI, a decision request and a complaint in the same email.
Be specific"By Thursday 5 pm" instead of "soon"; "p95 rose from 300 ms to 900 ms" instead of "it is slower".
Short words, short sentencesPlain language is a sign of command, not simplicity.
Confirm understandingAsk the other person to restate the decision, or send a one-line summary afterwards.
Assume good intent, check impactText lacks tone. Read twice before sending; if emotional, wait.
BLUF exampleWeak: "I was looking at the logs from last week and noticed a few things with the queue..." Strong: "Decision needed by Friday: upgrade the queue library (2 days) or accept weekly timeouts. I recommend upgrading. Details below."

Active listening and asking good questions Basic

Listening is the highest-leverage communication skill. People who feel heard cooperate; people who feel dismissed resist even correct ideas.

TechniqueWhat you doExample
ParaphraseRestate in your own words"So the main risk for you is the data migration, not the UI. Is that right?"
Open questionsStart with what / how / tell me about"What would a good outcome look like?"
Clarifying questionsMake vague words concrete"When you say 'fast', is that under one second for 95% of requests?"
SilenceWait 3-5 seconds after a questionQuiet people often add the most valuable point after a pause
Label emotionName what you notice"It sounds like the late change is frustrating."
Five whysAsk why repeatedly to reach the root"Why do you need that report? ... Why monthly?"
Listening anti-patternsPreparing your reply while the other person talks; interrupting to fix; answering a question that was not asked; checking your phone; jumping to solutions when the person wanted to be heard. Ask: "Do you want ideas, or do you just want me to listen?"

Email, chat and asynchronous writing Basic

Distributed teams run on writing. A message that stands alone saves several rounds of back-and-forth.

A message that stands alone

  • Subject / first line: purpose and action ("Decision needed: ...")
  • Context: two sentences, with links
  • Ask: exactly what you need, from whom, by when
  • Options and your recommendation
  • Next step if no reply

Choosing a channel

  • Chat: quick, low-stakes, perishable questions
  • Email / ticket: decisions, audit trail, external parties
  • Doc / wiki: anything people will search for later
  • Call: ambiguity, emotion, conflict, complex negotiation
  • Face to face: sensitive feedback
Subject: Decision needed by Fri 12 Jun - payment provider timeout

Summary: Checkout fails for ~2% of orders when the payment provider is slow.
Need: Approval to add a 3 s timeout with one retry (2 dev-days).
Why now: Black Friday traffic starts in 3 weeks.
Options:
  A) Timeout + retry (recommended) - 2 days, removes most failures
  B) Switch provider - 6 weeks, higher cost
  C) Do nothing - risk of ~400 failed orders per sale day
If I do not hear back by Friday I will start option A.
Details: link to ticket SHOP-482 and the failure graph.

Synchronous vs asynchronous

Use synchronous (meeting/call) whenUse asynchronous (doc/message) when
Topic is ambiguous or emotionalInformation is being shared, not debated
Real-time brainstorming helpsPeople are in different time zones
Conflict or sensitive feedbackYou want considered, written input from everyone
Quick decision needs many voicesA permanent record is needed
Chat etiquetteDo not send just "hi" and wait. Write "Hi, quick question about X: ... (no rush, reply when free)". Use threads, state urgency honestly, and avoid @here unless it affects everyone.

Running and attending effective meetings Intermediate

A meeting is expensive: 8 people for one hour is a full working day. Hold one only when it beats a message.

PhaseDo
BeforeState the purpose and desired outcome (decide / inform / brainstorm). Invite only people needed. Share the agenda and pre-reads. Cancel if the purpose is met in writing.
DuringStart on time; assign a note-taker and timekeeper; stick to the agenda; invite quieter people by name; park off-topic items in a "parking lot"; end with decisions, owners and dates.
AfterSend notes within a day: decisions, actions (owner + date), open questions. Notes are what makes the meeting count.

Meeting types and rhythm

TypeGoalTip
Stand-upSync on progress and blockers15 min, focus on the goal, not a status report to a manager
Planning / refinementAgree scope and clarify itemsPrepare ready items beforehand
Design reviewChallenge a proposalSend the doc first; discuss options, not people
1:1Relationship, growth, blockersPerson's agenda first; weekly or fortnightly
RetrospectiveImprove how the team worksEnd with 1-2 owned actions
Demo / reviewShow working resultsTell the user story, not the code
Decline politely"I do not think I can add value to this one. Could you send the outcome? I am happy to review the notes or join if my input on X is needed."
Part 2 — Technical communication

Technical writing: README, design docs, RFCs and ADRs Intermediate

Writing a design down forces clarity, lets people review asynchronously, and preserves the why that code alone cannot show.

DocumentPurposeContains
READMEOnboard a new developer in 10 minutesWhat it is, prerequisites, run, test, deploy, contact
Design doc / RFCPropose a significant change and gather feedbackProblem, goals/non-goals, options with trade-offs, recommendation, risks, rollout
ADR (Architecture Decision Record)Record one decision permanentlyContext, decision, consequences, status
RunbookHelp on-call fix a known problemSymptoms, checks, commands, escalation
Post-incident reviewLearn from failures without blameTimeline, impact, causes, actions
# ADR 007: Use a message queue for email sending

Status: Accepted (2026-03-02)

## Context
Sending email inside the request adds 800 ms and fails the whole order
when the provider is down.

## Decision
Publish an "OrderPlaced" message; a background worker sends the email,
with retries and a dead-letter queue.

## Consequences
+ Faster checkout, resilient to provider outages
- Eventual consistency; need monitoring on queue depth
- One more component to operate

## Alternatives considered
- Synchronous call with retry (rejected: still slows requests)
- Third-party transactional service only (rejected: no ordering control)
Good design docsState non-goals, show at least two real options, say what would make you change your mind, and keep it short enough to be read. Ask for specific reviewers and a deadline for comments.

Giving and receiving code review feedback Intermediate

Code review spreads knowledge and keeps quality high, but tone decides whether it builds or damages trust.

As a reviewer

  • Review the code, not the person
  • Ask questions: "What happens if the list is empty?"
  • Label severity: blocker, suggestion, nit, question
  • Explain why and offer an alternative
  • Praise good solutions
  • Automate style (linters) so humans review design and risk
  • Respond within a working day

As an author

  • Keep pull requests small (under about 400 lines)
  • Write a description: why, what, how to test, screenshots
  • Self-review before requesting
  • Assume good intent; thank reviewers
  • Reply to every comment (fixed / disagree + reason)
  • Do not take it personally; the code is not you
Instead ofSay
"This is wrong.""I think this could return null when the user has no orders. Should we handle that?"
"Why did you do it this way?""Help me understand the reason for a static list here. Would a lookup table be easier to extend?"
"Obviously you should use a dictionary.""suggestion: a dictionary keyed by id would make this O(1) instead of scanning the list."
Disagreement in reviewIf two comment rounds do not converge, stop typing and talk (call or pair). Agree on principle or let the owner decide. Do not let a PR sit for days over a style preference.

Status updates, bad news and escalation Intermediate

Stakeholders do not mind problems; they mind surprises. Share risk early, with options.

Status colourMeaningExample summary
GreenOn track, no help needed"Login rework: 80% done, release on Thursday."
AmberAt risk, recovery plan exists, awareness requested"Search is 3 days behind due to API change. Plan: drop facet filters from v1."
RedWill miss without a decision or help"Cannot meet 30 June without a second developer or removing reports. Need a decision by Monday."
Project: Customer portal      Status: AMBER

Done this week: login + password reset in staging, 40 unit tests added.
Next week:      profile page, accessibility fixes.
Risks:          identity provider sandbox is unstable (2 outages) - could slip 3 days.
Asks:           contact at the IdP who can raise priority (owner: Priya, by Wed).
Dates:          release still Fri 20 Jun; will confirm Wed.

How to deliver bad news

  1. Say it early and plainly: "We will miss the date."
  2. Give impact, not just cause.
  3. Bring options with cost and your recommendation.
  4. Say what you need and by when.
  5. Follow up with a written summary and updates on a fixed rhythm.

Escalation, done well

  • Try to resolve at the lowest level first; show what you tried.
  • Escalate problems with options, not complaints.
  • Tell the other party before you escalate ("no surprises").
  • Escalate on facts: impact, deadline, decision needed.
  • Use it for blocked decisions, resource conflicts, ethics/safety or repeated missed commitments.

Explaining technical topics to non-technical stakeholders Intermediate

Translate technical facts into business impact. Your audience cares about time, money, risk and customers.

Technical statementBusiness translation
"We have a lot of technical debt in the billing module.""New billing features currently take about twice as long, and bugs here have cost us three incidents this quarter. Two weeks of cleanup would cut that."
"The database needs an index.""Reports take 40 seconds. A small change will bring that to under 2 seconds."
"We need to upgrade the framework.""The current version loses security support in June. Without the upgrade we cannot pass the customer's audit."

Techniques

  • Analogy: "a cache is like keeping frequently used files on your desk"
  • Concrete numbers and examples
  • Layer the detail: one-sentence answer, then more if asked
  • Visuals: simple boxes and arrows
  • Avoid jargon or define it once

Avoid

  • Talking down or over-explaining
  • Hiding uncertainty: say "I am 80% sure, here is how we will confirm"
  • Saying "it depends" without saying on what
  • Overselling certainty in estimates
Part 3 — Feedback, conflict and negotiation

Feedback: the SBI model Intermediate

Useful feedback is specific, timely, observable and about impact. The SBI model keeps it factual: Situation, Behaviour, Impact, then ask.

PartWhatExample
SituationWhen and where"In Tuesday's design review..."
BehaviourWhat an observer could see or hear"...you interrupted Sam twice while she was explaining the cache design."
ImpactEffect on you, team or goal"She stopped contributing, and we missed her concern about invalidation."
InviteAsk for their view / next step"How did that feel to you? What could we try next time?"

Giving feedback well

  • Give it soon, privately for corrective, publicly for praise (if the person likes it)
  • One or two points at a time
  • Separate fact from interpretation
  • Agree next steps and follow up
  • Praise specifically: what they did and what it enabled

Receiving feedback well

  • Listen fully; do not defend or explain first
  • Say thanks and ask for an example
  • Summarise what you heard
  • Take time to decide what to act on
  • Close the loop later: "I tried X, did it help?"
Radical candour in one lineCare personally and challenge directly. Caring without challenging is "ruinous empathy"; challenging without caring is "obnoxious aggression".

Handling conflict and disagreement Intermediate

Task conflict ("which design?") improves decisions when handled respectfully. Relationship conflict ("I do not trust you") destroys teams. Aim to keep disagreement about the problem.

StyleApproachUse when
CollaborateBoth goals fully met, joint problem-solvingImportant to both, time available
CompromiseEach gives up somethingTime pressure, roughly equal power
AccommodateYieldThe issue matters more to them, or to preserve the relationship
CompetePush your positionSafety, ethics, emergencies
AvoidPostponeTrivial issue or emotions too high (return later)

A practical sequence

  1. Cool down if emotional; arrange a private conversation.
  2. Understand first: ask about their goals, concerns and constraints, and paraphrase them.
  3. Find the shared goal ("we both want a stable release").
  4. Separate positions from interests: "I want microservices" (position) versus "I want independent deployments" (interest).
  5. Generate options and agree on objective criteria (data, benchmark, prototype, customer impact).
  6. Decide: consensus where possible; otherwise the accountable owner decides after hearing all views. Disagree and commit.
  7. Record the decision (ADR/ticket) and revisit with data if needed.
"I think we see this differently and I would like to understand your view.
 My concern is the deployment risk on Friday. What is your main concern?
 ...
 It sounds like the date matters most to you, and the risk matters most to me.
 Could we release the low-risk part Friday and the rest Tuesday?"
Escalate when discussion is stuck after a fair attempt, the issue crosses a team boundary, or behaviour is disrespectful or unsafe. Tell the other person first and present both views fairly.

Negotiation, trade-offs and saying no Intermediate

Negotiation is finding a deal both sides can accept, not winning. In delivery work you negotiate scope, deadlines, priorities and resources daily.

ConceptMeaning
Interests, not positionsAsk "why do you need that?" to find flexible needs behind a fixed demand.
BATNAYour best alternative if no agreement. Know it before negotiating.
ZOPAThe zone where a deal is acceptable to both.
AnchoringThe first number sets the range. Make the first offer if you have good information.
Trade, do not concede"If we move the date, can we drop reports?" Always get something in return.
Objective criteriaBenchmarks, market data, past velocity.

Saying no without damaging the relationship

PatternExample
Yes, if..."Yes, we can add export if we move audit logging to next sprint."
Not now, here is when"I cannot take this before the release. I can start on the 24th."
Offer a smaller version"I cannot build the full dashboard by Friday; I can ship the three key charts."
Explain impact on current priorities"If I do this, the payment fix slips two days. Which do you prefer?"
Redirect"The platform team owns this. I will introduce you and share the context."
Negotiating salary or roleResearch the market range, state your value with evidence, give a range whose bottom is acceptable to you, discuss total compensation (bonus, equity, growth, flexibility), and stay positive and collaborative. Do not give a number first if you can ask for the budget.
Part 4 — Leading people

Leadership paths: senior engineer, tech lead, manager Intermediate

Leadership is influence, not title. You can lead as an individual contributor by raising the quality and speed of everyone around you.

RoleFocusSuccess looks like
Senior engineerSolve ambiguous problems; own a component end to end; raise the bar via reviewsTeam ships faster with fewer defects because of you
Tech leadTechnical direction, design decisions, unblocking, delivery of one team's workClear architecture, aligned team, predictable delivery
Engineering managerPeople: hiring, growth, performance, team health, processHigh-performing, retained, growing team
Staff / principal / architectCross-team strategy, standards and hard technical problemsMultiple teams succeed on a coherent technical direction

Managing vs leading

Management (do things right)

  • Plan, organise, track
  • Remove obstacles
  • Maintain stability and process

Leadership (do the right things)

  • Set direction and context
  • Motivate and align people
  • Drive change and growth
Servant leadershipThe leader's job is to make the team successful: provide clarity, remove blockers, protect focus, grow people and give credit away. Take responsibility when things go wrong, share credit when they go right.
The new-lead trapContinuing to be the best coder and the bottleneck. As scope grows, shift from "I solve it" to "I make sure it gets solved well": delegate, review, coach, and document.

Mentoring and coaching Intermediate

Mentoring shares experience and advice; coaching helps a person find their own answer by asking questions. Good leaders use both and choose by the person's skill and the situation.

Teaching / directingCoachingDelegating
Best forNew to the taskSome experience, building confidenceSkilled and motivated
YouShow, explain, give stepsAsk questions, let them tryAgree outcome, stay available

GROW coaching model

StepQuestion
Goal"What do you want to achieve?"
Reality"Where are you now? What have you tried?"
Options"What could you do? What else?"
Will"What will you do, and by when? What could stop you?"

Mentoring a junior developer

  • Pair on a real task, then step back
  • Give small, scoped, increasingly hard work
  • Explain why, not just what
  • Review code kindly; point to docs
  • Let them make safe mistakes
  • Set a time limit for being stuck (for example 30-60 min), then ask

Growing a strong engineer

  • Stretch assignments (lead a design, run a release)
  • Visibility: let them present
  • Sponsor, not just mentor: recommend them for opportunities
  • Agree a growth plan with 1-2 skills per quarter
Ask before answeringWhen someone brings a problem, try "What have you tried, and what do you think the cause is?" first. You build their problem-solving, and you learn what they already know.

Delegation, ownership and accountability Intermediate

Delegating is handing over outcome and authority, not just tasks. Done well it grows people and frees you for higher-leverage work.

LevelDescription
1. Do as I sayDetailed instructions
2. Research and reportInvestigate and bring options
3. RecommendPropose a decision; leader decides
4. Decide, then informAct, then tell me
5. Act on your ownFull ownership; report periodically

How to delegate

  1. Choose the right person (skill plus stretch).
  2. Explain the outcome and why it matters, with quality and deadline.
  3. State the authority level and constraints (budget, who to involve).
  4. Agree check-ins, not constant supervision.
  5. Provide support and resources; accept different approaches.
  6. Give credit, review learnings, adjust next time.

Extreme ownership habits

  • Own outcomes, not just assigned tickets
  • Say "I will" and follow through, or renegotiate early
  • Fix the problem, then fix the cause
  • Do not blame; describe facts and next steps

Delegation mistakes

  • Dumping tasks nobody wants
  • Delegating without authority
  • Micromanaging or taking the task back at first mistake
  • Never delegating because "I am faster"
RACIFor shared work clarify Responsible (does it), Accountable (one owner, final say), Consulted (two-way input), Informed (one-way). Exactly one A per item.

Psychological safety, motivation and team health Advanced

Research on high-performing teams (Google's Project Aristotle) found that psychological safety – believing you will not be punished for speaking up, asking, or admitting mistakes – was the strongest predictor of team success.

How leaders create it

  • Admit your own mistakes and uncertainty first
  • Ask for dissent: "What am I missing?"
  • Respond to bad news with curiosity, not blame
  • Blameless post-incident reviews
  • Thank people for raising problems
  • Hear every voice in meetings

How it gets destroyed

  • Public blame or sarcasm
  • Punishing the messenger
  • Only rewarding heroics
  • Ignoring concerns repeatedly
  • One loud voice dominating

What motivates people

Driver (Pink)MeaningLeader action
AutonomyControl over how and whatGive outcomes, not step lists
MasteryGetting better at something that mattersStretch work, learning time, feedback
PurposeKnowing why the work mattersConnect tasks to users and goals
Tuckman stageTeam behaviourLeader focus
FormingPolite, unsure of rolesClarity, goals, introductions
StormingConflict over approachCoach, set norms, keep it respectful
NormingAgreed ways of workingReinforce, build trust

Burnout signalsCynicism, withdrawal, quality slips, constant overtime, loss of humour. Respond with workload changes and a direct conversation, not a pep talk. Sustainable pace is a leadership responsibility.

Making and communicating decisions Advanced

Teams slow down when nobody knows who decides. Clear ownership plus transparent reasoning beats endless consensus.

ToolIdea
DACIDriver (runs the process), Approver (one decider), Contributors (give input), Informed.
One-way vs two-way doorsReversible decisions: decide quickly with about 70% of the information. Irreversible ones deserve more analysis.
Disagree and commitArgue hard before the decision, support it fully after.
Decision matrixList options, weight the criteria, score, and discuss the result (not a replacement for judgement).
Pre-mortemImagine the plan failed a year from now; list reasons; mitigate the top ones.
Time-boxed spike / prototypeBuy information cheaply when unknowns dominate.

Communicate a decision

  • What was decided and by whom
  • Why: options and trade-offs considered
  • What happens next and who owns it
  • How to give input or when it will be revisited

Cognitive biases to watch

  • Confirmation: seeking supporting evidence only
  • Sunk cost: continuing because of past effort
  • Anchoring: first number dominates
  • HiPPO: highest paid person's opinion wins
  • Groupthink: silence taken as agreement

Running 1:1s, retrospectives and hiring Advanced

PracticeHow
1:1 meetingWeekly or fortnightly, 30 min, the report's agenda first. Ask: "What is on your mind?", "What is blocking you?", "How can I help?", "What feedback do you have for me?", "Where do you want to grow?" Keep shared notes. Never cancel casually.
RetrospectiveSet the stage, gather data (what went well, what to improve), generate insights, decide 1-2 actions with owners, close. Rotate facilitator and formats; review last retro's actions first.
Performance conversationsNo surprises: continuous feedback, clear expectations, written examples, a plan with checkpoints and support. Separate coaching from evaluation.
Interviewing candidatesUse a structured scorecard; same questions for each candidate; behavioural plus technical; evidence over gut feel; sell the role honestly; give timely feedback.
OnboardingA 30-60-90 day plan, a buddy, a first small production change in week one, documentation to read, and early feedback.
Interviewer biasesSimilarity bias ("like me"), halo effect, first impressions, and judging by confidence rather than evidence. Write scores before discussing to avoid anchoring on the first opinion.
Part 5 — Influence, time and career

Influencing without authority and managing stakeholders Advanced

Most important changes require agreement from people who do not report to you. Influence is built from credibility, relationships and a clear case.

Time management, prioritisation and focus Basic

You cannot do everything. Leaders choose what not to do. The Eisenhower matrix sorts work by urgent and important.

UrgentNot urgent
ImportantQ1 Do now: outages, deadlinesQ2 Schedule: design, mentoring, automation, learning
Not importantQ3 Delegate/shorten: interruptions, some emailQ4 Drop: time wasters

Habits that work

  • Plan the top 1-3 outcomes each day
  • Block focus time (calendar) and silence notifications
  • Batch email and chat checks
  • Time-box research and be stuck for a limited time
  • Single-task; limit work in progress
  • Weekly review: what mattered, what to drop

Techniques

  • Pomodoro: 25 min focus, 5 min break
  • Eat the frog: hardest task first
  • Two-minute rule: do it now if it takes less than 2 min
  • Parkinson's law: work expands to fill the time, so set deadlines

Remote, hybrid and cross-cultural collaboration Intermediate

  • Default to writing: decisions in docs/tickets; summarise calls; make information discoverable.
  • Overlap hours: agree a core window for synchronous work; rotate inconvenient meeting times fairly.
  • Over-communicate context: say what you are working on, when you are away, and when you are blocked.
  • Camera and tone: use video for relationship-building and sensitive topics; add emoji or a clear tone marker in chat.
  • Cross-cultural: direct vs indirect styles, hierarchy, and idioms differ. Avoid slang, confirm understanding in writing, and invite quiet colleagues to contribute in writing.
  • Build trust: small social moments, demos, shared goals, and credit given publicly.
  • Hand-offs across time zones: end-of-day notes with state, next step and blockers so the next person can continue without waiting.
Working with other teamsLearn their priorities and constraints, agree an interface (API, SLA, contact), raise issues early with a proposed solution, and thank them publicly. Treat them as customers and partners, not obstacles.

AreaPractice

Learning planPick 1-2 skills per quarter tied to your goal; learn by building and teaching (talk, blog, internal session).

Part 6 — Interview preparation

Behavioural interviews: the STAR method Practice

Behavioural questions ("Tell me about a time...") test past behaviour as a predictor of future behaviour. STAR keeps answers structured and evidence-based.

LetterContentShare of answer
SituationBrief context: where, when, what was at stakeabout 15%
TaskYour specific responsibility or goalabout 10%
ActionWhat you did, step by step, and why (use "I")about 60%
ResultMeasurable outcome plus what you learnedabout 15%

Build a story bank of six

Conflict

Disagreement with a peer or stakeholder resolved with data or compromise.

Failure

A real mistake, your ownership, the fix and the lasting change.

Leadership

Led without authority, mentored, or rallied a team.

Tight deadline

Prioritised, cut scope, communicated early, delivered.

Technical decision

Trade-offs weighed, decision made, outcome measured.

Learning

Picked up an unfamiliar technology or domain quickly.

TipsTailor each story to the question. Be honest, keep to about two minutes, use real numbers, and end on the learning. If you only have "we" stories, practise isolating your personal contribution. For hypothetical questions, state your assumptions and the trade-offs, then pick an option.

Self-test quiz Practice

Ten scenario questions. Pick an answer, read the explanation, and keep score.

Leadership and communication interview questions Prep

Filter by topic, expand a question and answer it aloud before reading the guidance.

How do you explain a complex technical issue to a non-technical stakeholder? comm

Start from their goal, state the impact in business terms (time, cost, risk, customers), use one analogy and numbers, offer options with a recommendation, and check understanding by asking them to restate the decision. Give more detail only on request.

How do you keep stakeholders informed on a long project? comm

Agree the audience and cadence upfront; short written updates with status colour, progress, risks, asks and dates; demos each sprint; early flags for slips with options; a single source of truth (board/dashboard).

Tell me about a time you had to deliver bad news. comm

Choose a real case. Show you acted early, shared facts and impact, brought options and a recommendation, stayed calm, and followed through with regular updates. End with the outcome and what you changed to detect the issue sooner.

How do you run an effective meeting? comm

Clear purpose and outcome, only needed attendees, an agenda and pre-read, timekeeping, inclusion of quiet voices, parking lot for tangents, and notes with decisions and owners sent the same day. Cancel it if a message would do.

Describe a time you disagreed with your manager or a teammate. conf

Show respect and curiosity: you sought to understand their constraints, presented data or a prototype, proposed options, and accepted the final decision (disagree and commit) or changed your mind. Give the result and what you learned. Avoid painting the other person as wrong.

How do you give difficult feedback? conf

Promptly, privately, using SBI: situation, observable behaviour, impact; then ask for their view and agree on next steps and a follow-up. Be specific, kind and direct; do not bury it in praise or attack character.

How do you respond to criticism you disagree with? conf

Listen without defending, thank them, ask for examples, reflect on the part that may be valid, and then give your perspective calmly. Follow up later to show you acted on what was useful.

A team member is consistently underperforming. What do you do? conf

Find facts and causes first (clarity, skills, workload, personal issues). Have a private conversation with specific examples, clear expectations and support (pairing, training), agree a time-bound plan with check-ins, document progress, and involve your manager or HR if it does not improve. Stay fair and respectful throughout.

What does leadership mean to you, and how have you shown it without a title? lead

Setting direction, enabling others and taking ownership. Example angles: initiated a design doc or standard, mentored juniors, improved CI time, unblocked another team. Show the impact on others and the outcome.

How do you delegate and avoid micromanaging? lead

Match the task to the person's skill, explain the outcome, constraints and why, set authority level and check-ins, then let them choose the approach. Check results, not keystrokes, and coach instead of taking over.

How do you motivate a demotivated team? lead

Find the cause through 1:1s (unclear purpose, overload, recognition, conflict, tooling pain). Reconnect the work with user impact, give autonomy and growth opportunities, remove the top blockers, celebrate progress, and keep the pace sustainable.

How do you make a decision with incomplete information? lead

Decide whether it is reversible. If so, move quickly with an explicit assumption list and a way to measure; if not, time-box a spike or gather targeted data. Name the decider, communicate the reasoning, and set a date to review the outcome.

How do you mentor someone more junior? lead

Agree goals, pair on real work, then hand over progressively harder tasks. Ask questions to build thinking, review kindly with the "why", encourage safe failure, and review progress regularly.

How do you handle a team member who dominates discussions or never speaks? team

Use structure: round-robin, written input before discussion, time-boxed turns. Speak privately with the dominant person about the impact and with the quiet person about what helps them contribute. Build safety so all views are heard.

Tell me about a time you helped a struggling teammate. team

Show that you noticed, asked how to help, offered concrete support (pairing, resources, shifting tasks), respected their autonomy, and the outcome for the individual and the project.

How do you work with a team in a different time zone or culture? team

Write clear context-rich handovers, agree overlap hours, record decisions in shared docs, avoid idioms, confirm understanding explicitly, and invest in relationship-building video calls.

What do you do when priorities from two stakeholders conflict? team

Bring both together or to the Product Owner with the facts: value, deadlines, cost of delay and capacity. Let the decision owner prioritise, make the trade-off visible, and communicate the outcome to both.

Tell me about a failure and what you learned. self

Pick a real, moderate failure. Own it (no blame), explain the cause, how you contained it, and the lasting change (test, checklist, monitoring, process). Interviewers look for accountability and learning.

What are your strengths and weaknesses? self

Strengths: 2-3 relevant, each backed by evidence. Weakness: a real one that is not core to the job, plus concrete steps you are taking and progress so far. Avoid clichés such as "I work too hard".

How do you prioritise when everything is urgent? self

Clarify impact and deadlines, use urgent/important and cost-of-delay thinking, ask the decision owner to rank, communicate what will slip, and protect time for important non-urgent work to reduce future fires.

Where do you see yourself in 3-5 years? self

Describe growth in scope and impact (deeper technical ownership, mentoring or leading a team) that matches what the role offers, and name the skills you are building now. Show ambition without sounding like the role is a stepping stone.

Do you have any questions for us? self

Always ask. Examples: "What does success look like in the first 90 days?", "How does the team handle disagreement or incidents?", "How are decisions documented?", "What is the biggest challenge the team faces?", "How do people grow here?"
